Site Preparation for Concrete Core Cutting

What to prepare before CRTC arrives to cut concrete cores for compressive strength testing, so that the cores are valid and the structure is not damaged.

1. What core cutting does

A water-cooled diamond bit drills a cylinder of concrete from a member in the structure. The core is labeled, taken to CRTC, trimmed and capped, then tested for compressive strength under ASTM C39/C39M. Cores are used when cylinder results are low, when concrete quality is in doubt, or when the in-place strength must be assessed.

Coring is partly destructive. It leaves holes, can cut reinforcement, and the quality of the core depends on where and how it is drilled. Planning the locations carefully matters as much as the drilling.

3. Preparing each core location

  1. Agree and mark. Choose the locations with the responsible engineer and mark each one on the member, with an ID that matches your sketch or drawing.
  2. Choose sound locations. Avoid joints, edges, honeycombed or damaged areas unless those are what you want investigated. Avoid areas of highest stress where the engineer advises.
  3. Scan for steel and services. Locate reinforcement, conduits, pipes and ducts using drawings and a cover meter. Move the location if needed to keep the core clear of bars.
  4. Prepare the surface. Clean the area and remove loose finishes, coatings and debris so the rig can be fixed firmly.
  5. Provide access and support. Make sure the rig can be set up square to the surface on a stable base, and that people can reach the point safely.
  6. Provide water and power. Have a supply near the work area, with a way to drain or collect the slurry.

4. Information we need

  • Project, structure and member for each core
  • Member thickness and concrete grade
  • Age of the concrete and casting date
  • Reason for coring and the question to be answered
  • Number and diameter of cores required
  • Reinforcement drawings and service information
  • Whether the concrete will be dry or wet in service
  • Site contact name, phone and email

5. On site

  1. Arrival and induction. Your site contact meets the CRTC team, completes the induction and confirms the locations.
  2. Check. The technician checks each location again for steel and services before drilling.
  3. Drill. The rig is fixed square to the surface and the core is drilled with water cooling.
  4. Label. Each core is marked at once with its ID, location, top and drilling direction, and then wrapped to keep its moisture.
  5. Record. Length, any steel, voids or defects, and the date and time are logged for the report.
  6. Leave the site tidy. The holes are left clean for making good.

Do not load or modify the member in a way that changes the area being cored while the work is in progress, and keep people clear of the area below.

6. Limitations

  • Core strength is not the same as standard cylinder strength. The engineer interprets it together with the structure and specification.
  • Drilling can damage a core, and steel, voids or poor compaction in the core can lower or distort the result.
  • Thin members may not give cores long enough for a valid test.
  • A few cores give only local information. Use the number your specification or engineer requires.

7. Common problems

  • Locations not agreed with the engineer before the visit
  • No scan for steel and services, or no drawings available
  • No water or power at the work area
  • Unsafe access or an unstable base for the rig
  • No plan for slurry, or for making good the holes
  • Cores not labeled with location and direction

8. Frequently asked questions

Search, or filter by topic.

Who decides where cores are taken?

The engineer responsible for the structure should agree the locations, because coring leaves holes and can affect reinforcement. Mark them before CRTC arrives.

Can you core through reinforcement?

Avoid it. Locations are scanned for steel first and moved where possible. If a bar cannot be avoided, tell CRTC, because steel in the test portion affects the result and is reported.

What size of core will be taken?

The diameter depends on the maximum aggregate size and your specification. 100 mm and 150 mm are common. CRTC will confirm the size before coring.

Is there a minimum member thickness?

Yes. A core needs enough length for its diameter. Thin slabs or members may not give a valid compression test, so tell CRTC the thickness before coring.

Do you provide water and power?

The rig normally needs water for cooling and a power supply. Confirm with CRTC what they bring and what the site must provide.

Why is the direction of coring recorded?

Concrete strength can differ between the vertical and horizontal directions. The report states how each core was drilled relative to the member.

What happens to the holes?

The holes remain after coring and must be made good, normally with a suitable repair material. Agree who does this and when before the visit.

What if the drill meets steel or voids?

The operator stops, records what was found and, where possible, moves to a nearby location agreed with the engineer. Damaged cores may be rejected or tested with a note.

Why is core strength different from cylinder strength?

Cores come from the structure, with its own compaction and curing, and drilling can affect them. The two are different measures and are interpreted by the engineer, not simply compared.

What will the report contain?

Core ID, location, orientation, diameter, length, moisture condition, any reinforcement or defects, correction applied, and compressive strength to the nearest 0.1 MPa.

How many cores do I need?

Follow your specification or the engineer’s instruction. Some codes ask for several cores per questionable area. Ask CRTC if you are unsure.

When will I get the results?

Cores may need trimming, end preparation and moisture conditioning before testing, so allow time. CRTC will quote turnaround, normally [X] working days.

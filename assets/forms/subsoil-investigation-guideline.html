Site Preparation for Subsoil Investigation

What to prepare before CRTC arrives to drill boreholes and carry out Standard Penetration Tests and sampling by wash boring, so that the investigation is safe, complete and on time.

1. What the investigation does

Boreholes are advanced into the ground, commonly by wash boring, and a Standard Penetration Test is done at regular depths. A split-spoon sampler is driven by a 63.5 kg hammer dropped 760 mm, and the blows needed for the last 300 mm of a 450 mm drive give the N-value. Disturbed samples come from the sampler, and undisturbed samples may be taken in soft clays as your specification requires. Groundwater levels are recorded.

The results are used by a geotechnical engineer to understand the soil profile and to recommend foundations. The investigation is only as good as the site preparation, because poor access, missing services information or no water supply cause delays and unsafe working.

3. Preparing each borehole location

  1. Mark the location. Mark each borehole with a peg or paint and an ID, and show it on a site plan with coordinates or distances from fixed points.
  2. Check for services. Confirm that no buried pipes or cables, and no overhead lines, are within the working area of the rig.
  3. Clear the area. Remove vegetation, debris, loose fill and old foundations. Level the ground where the rig will stand.
  4. Make access firm. Provide a safe route for the rig and crew, with a firm base or mats where the ground is soft.
  5. Prepare water and drainage. Arrange a water supply close by and a pit or channel for the returned water and slurry, kept away from neighbours and drains.
  6. Make it safe. Set barriers, signs and lighting, and arrange security for equipment left overnight.

4. Information we need

  • Project, address and the proposed structure
  • Site plan, with borehole locations and ground levels
  • Number and depth of boreholes required
  • Tests and samples required, and the standards to follow
  • Service drawings and known hazards
  • Previous investigation reports or known fill history
  • Access arrangements, working hours and permits
  • Site contact name, phone and email

5. On site

  1. Arrival and induction. Your site contact meets the CRTC team, completes the induction and confirms the borehole locations.
  2. Setup. The rig is set up level over each location and the water supply and slurry pit are checked.
  3. Drilling and testing. The borehole is advanced, and SPT and sampling are carried out at the required depths. The soil is described as it is recovered.
  4. Groundwater. The water level is recorded during drilling and after a stabilization period, as agreed.
  5. Samples. Samples are labeled with the borehole, depth and date, sealed to retain moisture, and stored safely.
  6. Completion. The borehole is finished at the required depth or at refusal, then backfilled or made safe, and the area is left tidy.

Keep people, vehicles and other work away from the rig and the borehole while drilling and testing. A falling hammer and rotating rods are hazards.

6. Limitations

  • Boreholes give information only at the points drilled. Soil can change between them.
  • SPT N-values depend on the equipment and method. They are indicators, not direct measures of bearing capacity.
  • Wash boring and sampling disturb the soil. Gravel, boulders, fill and very dense layers can stop progress or affect the results.
  • Groundwater readings change with the season and the drilling method.

7. Common problems

  • Borehole locations not marked, or not matching the plan
  • No service information, or services struck during drilling
  • Soft or flooded access that the rig cannot use
  • No water supply or no place for slurry
  • Number and depth of boreholes not agreed before the visit
  • No level reference, so ground levels cannot be reported

8. Frequently asked questions

How many boreholes do I need, and how deep?

It depends on the structure, the foundation type, the site area and your specification or code. Agree this with your geotechnical or structural engineer before the investigation, and tell CRTC.

Why must underground services be checked?

Drilling can damage buried pipes and cables, which is dangerous and costly. Provide service drawings, and confirm with the utility owners before drilling.

Why is water needed?

Wash boring uses water pumped down the drill rods to carry cuttings up the borehole. Without a reliable supply, drilling slows or stops. Confirm what CRTC brings.

What if the site is flooded or very soft in the monsoon?

Tell CRTC early. A rig cannot work safely on soft ground, and access may need to be prepared with timber mats or a firm track. The visit may need rescheduling.

Can you drill inside an existing building?

Sometimes, with a smaller rig, but it needs clear headroom, access, ventilation and permission. Tell CRTC the conditions before the visit.

What is the SPT?

The Standard Penetration Test drives a split-spoon sampler by dropping a 63.5 kg hammer 760 mm. The blows needed for the last 300 mm of a 450 mm drive give the N-value, an indicator of soil strength or density.

How often are tests and samples taken?

Your specification sets this. Commonly SPT is done at regular depth intervals and at each change of soil, with disturbed samples recovered from the split spoon.

When do you stop drilling?

At the depth set in your specification, or earlier if refusal is met, for example on rock, boulders or very dense layers. CRTC records the reason for stopping.

Is groundwater recorded?

Yes. The water level is noted while drilling and again after a stabilization period, because the first reading can be disturbed by the drilling water. Agree the observation times with CRTC.

What will the report contain?

Borehole logs with soil description and N-values, sample depths, groundwater levels, borehole locations and levels, and any observations during drilling. Laboratory results and recommendations may be separate.

Does the SPT give the bearing capacity?

Not directly. N-values and laboratory tests are used by a geotechnical engineer, with the structure details, to recommend foundation type and allowable bearing pressure.

What happens to the boreholes afterwards?

Boreholes are backfilled or made safe as agreed. Tell CRTC if they should be left open for groundwater observation.

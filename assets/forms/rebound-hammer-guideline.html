Site Preparation for Rebound Hammer Test

What to prepare before CRTC arrives to test your concrete with the rebound hammer, so that the readings are reliable and the visit is not wasted.

1. What the test does

A spring-loaded mass in the hammer strikes the concrete through a plunger and rebounds. The distance it rebounds is the rebound number, which depends on the hardness of the concrete near the surface. The test is quick, non-destructive and useful for checking uniformity across a member or between members.

It tests the surface layer only, so it cannot show the strength of the whole member. The result depends heavily on the surface, which is why site preparation matters.

3. Preparing each test area

  1. Choose and mark. Pick a sound area away from edges, corners, joints and obvious defects. Mark it with an ID that matches your plan.
  2. Remove finishes. Strip plaster, paint, tiles and coatings down to bare concrete across the whole test area.
  3. Make it smooth. Grind rough or uneven spots with an abrasive stone until the surface is flat and hard, then brush off the dust.
  4. Check for steel. Locate reinforcement near the area so that readings can be placed away from bars.
  5. Support the member. Make sure slender or thin members are rigid and cannot move under the blow.
  6. Record the condition. Note whether the surface is dry, damp or wet, and whether it is exposed or protected, so the report can state it.

4. Information we need

  • Project, structure and member for each test area
  • Concrete age, grade, mix and cement type, where known
  • Type of formwork and surface finish
  • Curing history and exposure, for example indoor or outdoor
  • Purpose of the test and the question to be answered
  • Number of test areas and member positions
  • Whether strength is to be estimated and what calibration is available
  • Site contact name, phone and email

5. On site

  1. Arrival and induction. Your site contact meets the CRTC team, completes the induction and confirms the test areas.
  2. Hammer check. The technician checks the hammer on its test anvil before use, as the method requires.
  3. Testing. Several readings are taken in each area, with the hammer held square to the surface. The direction of the hammer is noted.
  4. Review. Readings are checked and outliers handled as the standard requires. An area may be re-tested after further surface preparation.
  5. Records. Area IDs, directions, surface condition and results are logged for the report.

Keep people clear of the area below and beside the test position, and use a stable platform when testing above head height.

6. Limitations

  • The test measures surface hardness over a shallow depth, not the strength of the whole member.
  • Results are affected by surface condition, moisture, carbonation, aggregate type, age and the support of the member.
  • Strength can only be estimated with a calibration for the same concrete, and the estimate is approximate.
  • It is not a substitute for cylinder or core strength testing where these are required.

7. Common problems

  • Testing over plaster, paint or other finishes
  • Rough, scaled or honeycombed surfaces that were not ground
  • Readings over reinforcement or near edges
  • Thin or loose members that move under the blow
  • Wet surfaces, with no record of the condition
  • No calibration data, but a strength value expected

8. Frequently asked questions

What does the rebound hammer measure?

It measures the surface hardness of the concrete from how far a spring-driven mass rebounds. The rebound number is related to strength, but only when it is calibrated for the concrete tested.

Does the plaster or paint need to be removed?

Yes. The hammer must strike bare, sound concrete. Finishes and coatings absorb the impact and lower the reading.

Why must the surface be smooth?

A rough or uneven surface changes the way the plunger strikes and gives scattered readings. Grinding with an abrasive stone gives a flat, hard surface.

Why locate the reinforcement first?

A bar close to the surface under the plunger raises the reading. Scanning lets CRTC place the readings away from steel.

Can slender members be tested?

Only if they are firmly supported. A thin slab or beam that moves or vibrates under the blow absorbs energy and gives low, unreliable readings.

What does the hammer direction matter?

Gravity changes the rebound number depending on whether the hammer points up, down or sideways. CRTC records the direction and applies the correction the method requires.

How many readings are taken?

Several readings are taken at each test area and the average rebound number is used, with outliers handled as the standard requires. The number is set by the method.

Does moisture or carbonation affect the result?

Yes. A wet surface tends to give lower readings, and a carbonated surface can give higher ones. Record what you know about the surface condition.

Can the test be done in rain?

Not on a wet surface. Provide cover and allow it to drain, or reschedule.

Does the test give the compressive strength?

Not directly. It gives rebound numbers. Strength can only be estimated with a calibration for the same concrete, usually from cores taken nearby, and the estimate is approximate.

Can the result be used for acceptance?

The rebound hammer is normally used to check uniformity and to compare areas. It does not replace cylinder or core strength testing for acceptance.

What will the report contain?

Test area IDs and locations, hammer direction, surface condition, individual and average rebound numbers, and any strength estimate with its basis and limitations.

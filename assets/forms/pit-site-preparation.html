Site Preparation for Pile Integrity Test (PIT)

What to prepare before CRTC arrives to test your piles with the Novatest P8000, so that the signals are clear and the visit is not wasted.

1. What the test does

A technician taps the pile head with a hand-held hammer and records the stress wave that travels down the pile and reflects back. The signal shows the pile’s continuity and indicates its length or the depth of any anomaly, such as a change in section or concrete quality. It is quick and non-destructive, but it does not measure load capacity.

The quality of the signal depends heavily on the pile head. Most poor results come from a rough, wet, cracked or contaminated surface, not from the instrument.

3. Preparing the pile head

  1. Cut to sound concrete. Trim to the cut-off level and remove laitance and any weak or contaminated concrete until the surface is hard and sound.
  2. Make the surface flat. Chip or grind high spots so the top is level and smooth in the area where the sensor and hammer will be used.
  3. Clean it. Remove dust, slurry, mud, oil and loose material. Wash if needed and let the surface drain.
  4. Keep it dry. Remove standing water. Cover the head if rain is expected.
  5. Manage the reinforcement. Arrange protruding bars so they do not block the test positions. CRTC will tell you where positions are needed.
  6. Mark the pile. Paint the pile ID clearly beside the pile and keep it matched to your pile schedule.

4. Information we need for each pile

  • Pile ID and location on the layout plan
  • Pile type and method of construction
  • Diameter or section size
  • Design length and as-built length
  • Cut-off level and ground level
  • Casting date and concrete grade
  • Reinforcement and cage details
  • Concreting notes, such as delays, temporary casing, overbreak or obstructions

5. On test day

  1. Arrival and induction. Your site contact meets the CRTC team, completes the induction and confirms the piles to be tested.
  2. Surface check. The technician inspects each pile head. Heads that do not meet the requirements are noted.
  3. Testing. The sensor is coupled to the pile head and several hammer impacts are recorded on the tablet. More than one position is used on larger piles.
  4. Data check. The signals are reviewed on site for quality. A pile may be re-tested after cleaning or re-positioning.
  5. Records. Pile ID, date, positions and any remarks are logged for the report.

Stop piling rigs, breakers and other vibrating plant near the pile being tested while readings are taken. Vibration and noise can mask the signal.

6. Limitations

  • The test does not give the load capacity of a pile.
  • The wave weakens with depth, so very long or slender piles, and piles in stiff soils, can be harder to read. CRTC will advise before testing.
  • Abrupt section changes, cracks, and variations in soil can produce signals that need interpreting alongside the pile records.
  • An anomaly is an indication, not a diagnosis. Follow-up investigation may be recommended.

7. Common problems

  • Testing before the concrete has reached the required age
  • Pile heads left rough, wet, muddy or not cut to sound concrete
  • Piles unmarked, or IDs that do not match the schedule
  • No pile records on site
  • Plant running next to the pile during testing
  • Unsafe or obstructed access to the pile head

8. Frequently asked questions

How long after casting can piles be tested?

Follow your specification. Many specifications ask for a minimum of about 7 days, so the concrete is strong enough to give a clear signal. Confirm the requirement with CRTC.

Must the pile be cut to cut-off level first?

Yes, preferably. Testing on a trimmed head of sound concrete gives the clearest signal. If a pile cannot be cut yet, say so on the pile record so the report can state it.

Does the reinforcement need to be removed?

No, but the test positions on the pile head must be clear of bars. CRTC will tell you the number and layout of positions for your pile diameter.

Can you test a pile with water or mud on top?

Not reliably. Remove standing water, mud and oil, and leave a clean, flat, dry area for the sensor.

What equipment will CRTC use?

The Novatest P8000 wireless pile integrity tester, which uses a wireless accelerometer sensor, a tablet and a hammer. Equipment may vary with the project, so ask CRTC.

How many piles can be tested in a day?

It depends on access, spacing and how well the piles are prepared. Ask CRTC for an estimate for your site, and have piles ready in testing order.

What if it rains?

Provide cover for the pile head, sensor and tablet. Testing may be paused or rescheduled if conditions affect the quality of the signal or safety.

What if my piles are not ready on the day?

Tell CRTC as early as you can. Piles that do not meet the preparation requirements may be skipped or rescheduled. Any extra visit may be chargeable under [CRTC policy].

Does the test give the load capacity of the pile?

No. PIT is a low-strain integrity test. It assesses continuity and shows the pile length or the depth to anomalies. Capacity needs load testing or other methods.

What will the report contain?

Pile ID, date, equipment, the recorded signals, the wave speed used, the estimated length or anomaly depth, and remarks on test quality and limitations.

What happens if an anomaly is reported?

A qualified engineer reviews the signal with the pile records. Further investigation, such as coring or other tests, may be recommended before a decision is made.

Are there pile types or lengths that are harder to test?

Yes. Very long or slender piles, piles in stiff soils, and piles with abrupt section changes can give weaker or harder-to-read signals. CRTC will advise before testing.
